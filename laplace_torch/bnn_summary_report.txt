# Comparative Analysis of BNN Implementations 

This document synthesizes the core methodologies, configurations, and performance metrics from the experimental notebooks, focusing specifically on Bayesian parameters for regression tasks. It is formatted for easy adaptation into a LaTeX report.

## 1. Introduction: BNNs for Regression Tasks
The primary focus of this analysis is the evaluation of different Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) implementations applied to regression tasks. While deterministic Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimations serve as a foundational baseline, the emphasis lies in quantifying epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty through diverse BNN strategies, specifically contrasting post-hoc approximations (Laplace-Torch) with learned variational posteriors (Bayesian-Torch).

## 2. BNN Implementations and Configurations

### 2.1 Laplace Approximation (Laplace-Torch)
* Formulation: A post-hoc method that fits a Gaussian distribution \(\mathcal{N}(w_{MAP}, H^{-1})\) around a pre-trained MAP estimate.
* Hessian Approximations: 
  * Full Hessian: Computes the full covariance matrix over parameters (e.g., used in Last-Layer Laplace for the final regression head).
  * Diagonal Hessian: Assumes independence between weights for greater scalability on larger parameter spaces.
* Inference Pipeline: Utilizes a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) predictive approach for highly efficient, closed-form predictive variance.
* Hyperparameter Tuning: Prior precision and noise standard deviation (\(\sigma\)) are optimized iteratively by minimizing the Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) / marginal likelihood on validation sets.

### 2.2 Stochastic Variational Inference (Bayesian-Torch)
* Formulation: Learns a variational posterior alongside the network weights natively during the training loop.
* Estimators: Employs Stochastic Variational Inference (SVI) with **Flipout** layers to de-correlate gradients within mini-batches and lower variance during Monte Carlo (MC) sampling.
* Initialization: Uses MOPED to initialize the posterior mean directly from the pre-trained MAP weights, significantly stabilizing training.
* Training Objective: Fine-tuned by maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), explicitly balancing Mean Squared Error (MSE) and the KL-divergence penalty. 
* Inference Pipeline: Dependent on Monte Carlo sampling (e.g., 50-100 passes) to approximate predictive distributions.

## 3. Performance Metrics and Computational Overhead

### 3.1 Predictive Quality and Calibration
* Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL): The primary metric used to evaluate the quality of the predictive probability distribution. Lower NLL indicates better captured uncertainty bounds.
* Regression Calibration Error: Measures the reliability of the predicted uncertainty intervals against actual empirical coverage.
* RMSE: Tracked to ensure point-prediction accuracy does not degrade unacceptably when introducing Bayesian stochasticity compared to the MAP baseline.

### 3.2 Computational Efficiency & Overhead
* Training Time Overhead: 
  * Laplace-Torch requires standard MAP training followed by a relatively fast post-hoc Hessian computation and inversion (especially fast when constrained to the Last-Layer).
  * Bayesian-Torch incurs a continuous overhead per epoch during ELBO fine-tuning due to weight sampling, making it comparatively more expensive to train.
* Inference Latency:
  * Laplace's GLM predictive formulation offers fast, closed-form evaluation.
  * Bayesian-Torch (Flipout) requires multiple forward MC passes (e.g., 100 samples) per input, substantially increasing inference latency and memory footprint proportional to the sample size.

## 4. Summary
The evaluation directly bridges the theoretical trade-offs between these two packages: prioritizing highly scalable, rapidly fitted closed-form estimations via Laplace-Torch versus deeply learned, flexible variational posteriors via Bayesian-Torch with Flipout, benchmarked empirically on training overhead, NLL, and calibration bounds.
